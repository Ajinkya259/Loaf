Loaf · colour, take two

The problem wasn't the hues.

Every render before this used soft studio lighting, which smears grey gradients across flat voxel faces and makes any palette look muddy. Fixing the light first, then choosing colour on top of it — and leaving naturalistic cat colours behind, because that's what made the last set feel obvious. Orange earns its place on merit: it is the one body colour that never loses against a light desktop, which is exactly where white fails.

Same colours. Only the light changed.

This is the change that matters most, whichever palette wins.

before

Soft three-point studio

Area lights wrap around every block, so each face carries a gradient. Reads as a generic 3D render of a cat rather than as designed voxel art — and on white fur the gradients turn to mud.

after

Hard axis suns, no shadows

Three suns straight down the axes, shadows off, specular killed. Every face takes exactly one flat value — top bright, front mid, side dark. That three-tone step is the voxel look; no palette substitutes for it.

Why not naturalistic

Biology is the obvious move

White, ginger, grey, tuxedo, siamese — every one is a colour a real cat comes in, which is exactly why the set felt predictable. Pulling the palette from product design instead is the whole point of this pass.

The trick in porcelain

Shadows don't have to be grey

Porcelain keeps the white body but shifts its shadow tone to cool blue instead of warm grey. That single move is what stops white reading as unpainted, and it's why it feels different from the white you've been looking at all along.

Still true

Dark points are structural

Whatever the body colour, the near-black tail, ears and feet are what hold her silhouette together against a light desktop. They're load-bearing, not decoration — which is why every palette here keeps them.
